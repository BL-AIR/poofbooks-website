The story

In the heat and steam of a Moroccan hammām, two men meet where anonymity makes desire possible. Ayman is practical, watchful, newly honest with himself and not yet honest with everyone else. Lucas is beautiful, wealthy, controlled — a man who has learned to make damage look like elegance.

What begins as a brief encounter becomes something neither of them can keep contained. From Morocco to Gibraltar, from the decks of the Phoenix to the snowfields of Switzerland and the ruinous appetites of Berlin, Ayman and Lucas are drawn into each other in ways that expose every imbalance between them: money and pride, secrecy and truth, care and control.

But Lucas has a past that still knows how to call him back. Lazarus understands the part of him that believes surrender must hurt. And Ayman, loving him, must face the harder question: whether protecting a man can become another way of possessing him.

The Art of Surrender is a sensual, psychologically charged novel about what we mistake for love, and what it costs to be truly seen, and still misunderstood.

Larry’s reviewOur review

Okay, sit down a minute, man, because this one got under my skin.

On the surface this is a love story that starts in a Moroccan bathhouse. Underneath, it’s about a guy who’s spent his whole life being useful, finally asking what it might feel like to be wanted. Ayman’s the good son, raised behind the counter of his old man’s chandlery in Gibraltar, just out of seven years with a woman who wanted him to shave his chest. He lands in El Jadida with a plan, a phrasebook’s worth of nerve, and no clue. Then along comes Lucas: Swiss, beautiful manners, bruises he won’t talk about. He takes Ayman by the hand, literally, and leads him down into the dark.

And that bathhouse, man. You can smell it. The sea breathes through the tunnels, the steam swallows guys whole and spits them back out. The building’s got an appetite of its own, and the writing makes the place come alive.

What really got me, though, is consent. Lucas gives him a safe word before they go down. One stranger reads Ayman’s hesitation and walks away kind; another one doesn’t. Learning to say yes is a skill, learning to say no is too, and the book treats both like they mean somethin. Beautiful.

Now, it’s not perfect. The shy newcomer and the worldly stranger, we’ve been down that road before. Lucas can seem a little too perfect early on; I kept waiting for him to trip over something. And now and then Ayman’s thoughts spell out what the scene already showed you. The money too: yachts, Porsches. Groovy, sure, but sometimes the book enjoys the fantasy more than it questions it.

It’s Blair Purvis’s first novel, and it travels. Boy does it travel: Morocco, across the Med, into the cold rooms of European money, in half a dozen languages.

Now listen. If explicit sex makes you squirm, this isn’t your trip. It starts early, in a dark basement, with more than two guys, and later it goes to some rough places: power, pain, real violence. Read the warning up front. But if you like your romance slow, sexy and asking hard questions? Pull up a cushion.

Larry’s take on a review we commissioned.

On the surface, this is a love story that begins in a Moroccan bathhouse. Underneath, it is a book about a man who has spent his whole life being useful and finally asks what it would feel like to be wanted.

Ayman is the dutiful son of a Lebanese family in Gibraltar. He was raised behind the counter of his father’s chandlery, and he’s fresh out of a seven-year relationship with a woman who wanted him to shave his chest. He arrives in El Jadida with a plan, a phrasebook’s worth of nerve and no idea what he’s doing. Lucas, a Swiss stranger with impeccable manners and bruises he won’t explain, takes him by the hand, quite literally, and leads him into the dark.

What drew me in was the feeling that everyone in the book is negotiating terms. Lucas offers safety the way some men offer a drink at a bar, and you sense early that his generosity is also a way of staying in charge. Ayman, meanwhile, is learning that saying yes is a skill, and so is saying no. The settings do a lot of the talking: steam, seawater, a riyāḍ behind thick walls, then a yacht and the chillier rooms of the European elite. The further the two men travel, the more the book asks who in this relationship is giving, and who is being given away.

The bathhouse scenes are the triumph. I came away able to smell the place. The ḥammām works like a living body: the sea “breathing” through the sluice tunnel, the boom of each wave arriving a beat before the breeze, steam that swallows men and gives them back. The building seems to have its own appetite, and the writing lets it set the rhythm of the sex.

I was also moved by how carefully the book handles consent. Lucas gives Ayman a safe word before they go down. One stranger reads Ayman’s hesitation and kindly walks away; another doesn’t. Ayman’s “No!” comes out too loud for the room, and he is embarrassed by it. That felt true. Not knowing how to say yes is treated as seriously as needing to say no. And there are the small intrusions of home: his father’s bleach, his mother’s rosewater soap, his grandmother’s word for “the lazy one”. Each memory turns up at exactly the wrong moment, and Ayman shuts it out. Without anyone saying so, you understand what he has come all this way to get free of.

My reservations are small, and they come from a reader who was already won over. The shy newcomer led into gay life by a worldly, beautiful stranger is a story queer fiction has told many times. The book knows that, and the bruises on Lucas’s back promise something darker, but in the opening chapters Lucas can seem almost too perfect: patient, wise, generous, fluent in every language and always one step ahead. I sometimes wanted him to put a foot wrong. Ayman’s quoted thoughts are often charming, but now and then they say out loud what the scene has already shown. The first night ends on “This is who I am”, and by then I already knew. Lucas’s careful, contraction-free English is clearly deliberate, and it does place him, but over long stretches of dialogue it can make him sound stiff when the scene wants him loose. And the money: the riyāḍ, the borrowed Porsche, the superyacht. The glamour is seductive, and that may be the point, yet at moments the book seemed to enjoy the fantasy a little more than it questioned it.

This is Blair Purvis’s debut novel. His author note calls him a passionate traveller and a proud gay dad, and the book feels like the work of someone who has spent a lot of time in other people’s countries and languages. The dialogue moves between English, Moroccan Darija, Levantine Arabic, French, German and Gibraltar’s own Llanito. Purvis leaves the foreign speech on the page and translates it in footnotes, so the characters can keep secrets from each other but never from the reader. The setting is rooted in a real place: El Jadida’s Portuguese Cistern, a flooded, vaulted Manueline chamber, is a genuine tourist landmark. The novel slyly sends its tourists there, and takes its lovers somewhere far less respectable, a fictional basement labyrinth that sounds very like the cistern’s darker twin. The two men take turns, each of the six Parts belonging to one of them, as the journey moves from Morocco across the Mediterranean into Europe and beyond.

This is for readers who like their romance slow-burning, sensual and morally complicated. It will suit anyone who loved Garth Greenwell’s frankness or Alan Hollinghurst’s eye for money and class, and who would rather a love story asked hard questions than handed out comfort. Travel lovers will enjoy the steam rooms, harbours and mountain houses too. Stay clear if explicit gay sex makes you uneasy: it arrives early, in a dark basement, with more than two men present, and the book goes on to rougher places involving power, pain and violence, with injury and blood. If you want reassurance on every page, this isn’t your book. It opens with a content warning; take it seriously.

The first page

The wooden door was ancient and heavy. Ayman pressed his shoulder into the time-weathered grain. It resisted, as though begging him to reconsider entering. The air inside was warmer than expected, heavy with the smell of saline, mould, and humidity. A woman dressed in black stood behind a broad wooden counter that marked the boundary between her world and the clientele’s. She was tending to a short, grey-haired man in his sixties, portly, barefoot, and wrapped in a towel. Ayman stopped just inside the doorway. He could turn around now, and no one would think the lesser of him.

The woman’s pen ran steadily down the open register. The older man leaned across the counter and protested in a hushed voice. She shook her head and flipped the page, checking each ledger entry with deliberate scrutiny. Ayman took a few steps forward, keeping a safe distance behind the man, who was becoming insistent. The Arabic sign above the woman in black read:

‘Ḥammām de Maroc. Women: Sun, Mon, Tue; Men: Wed, Thu, Sat.’

It was the place he had come for, not the one the tourists were steered towards. They went up the road to the Portuguese Cistern; this ḥammām barely registered online, half-forgotten, its renovations stalled somewhere short of respectable. Ayman suspected that what the restoration budget could not provide, the ḥammām’s reputation now quietly did.

Ayman had spent the last year imagining this moment in safer places. In the privacy of his bedroom, on late nights when sleep refused him, he had pictured encounters with faceless men, broad-shouldered, confident, and commanding, not portly and middle-aged. The ḥammām in his dreams did not smell of seawater. Of sweat, perhaps, but not like this.

Ayman wondered what he would say when it was his turn to be seen. He tried the phrases quietly in his head.

‘Addēsh et-tazkara?’

‘No, that sounds formal. Too formal.’

‘What would they say here? The vowels heavier. The consonants swallowed. In Morocco, the words are different. Bshḥal not addēsh.’

Would they hear the Levant in him? Or the Llanito lilt in his English? Would they hear he did not quite belong anywhere?

He tried again. ‘Ana… No, too stiff. Would she speak English? French? Plenty of tourists in El Jadida, so probably. Hopefully.’

The woman looked up from her register and acknowledged his presence with a nod, fixing him in place. The heavy door creaked open, and he heard the footsteps of a man joining the queue behind him.

He should have prepared properly. Entry fees? Towels? Protocol? Had he misunderstood everything, and was his kitchen-table Arabic sufficient to get answers?

The man at the counter was concluding his complaint; he was next in line. Ayman turned to leave.

Between Ayman and the door stood a man close to his own age, European in appearance with three-day stubble, denim shirt, and jeans, striking enough that Ayman’s urge to leave faltered.

Blair Purvis

Blair is an author of literary fiction whose work lives at the intersection of desire, geography, and the slow, difficult business of being known. Drawn to stories that cross borders, emotional as much as physical, Blair writes characters who travel far in search of the things they are most afraid to find in themselves.

A passionate traveller and proud gay dad, Blair brings to the page a deep curiosity about the lives men live privately versus the lives they perform publicly, and the particular tenderness and damage that accumulates in the space between the two.

Blair lives in Melbourne with his family and two cats.
